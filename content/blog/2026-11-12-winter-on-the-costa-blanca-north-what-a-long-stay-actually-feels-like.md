Pull up a lounger (and a blanket if you’re coming from the UK).

If you’ve only ever seen Dénia, Jávea, Calpe or Moraira in the middle of August, you already know the drill: beaches rammed, 35-plus degrees, and half an hour minimum for a table anywhere near the port. 

***But have you ever considered coming in the months of winter?.***

Winter here is a completely different place. Those of us who live on the Costa Blanca North year-round quietly think it’s the best time of all.

From November to March, the crowds vanish. The promenade is mostly dog walkers, early-morning joggers and the odd person in summer shorts and beach-ready bags. You can sit outside with a café con leche in January and not need a thick coat (unless you’re Spanish, in which case a scarf will make an appearance).

Whether you’re escaping the grey for 90 days, testing the waters before thinking about moving to the Marina Alta, or rolling up in a van, this is what winter actually feels like here, and the long-stay deals worth knowing about while they’re still around.

**Why the Costa Blanca North works so well in winter**

Unlike parts of the southern coast that either shut down completely or turn into empty concrete resort towns, the Marina Alta stays genuinely alive because these are real working towns.

* The weather: Crisp, sunny days averaging 16–20°C, bright skies, and beaches you can actually walk on. Local reality check: once the sun drops around 5:30 pm, the temperature falls fast, and those Spanish-tiled floors get cold. Pack layers and decent slippers. You’ll thank me.
* A slower rhythm: Restaurants have time to chat, parking is free and easy in spots that are impossible in July, and the weekly markets feel relaxed rather than like a contact sport.
* Walking and cycling: Without the summer humidity, this is prime time for Montgó, the coastal path around Moraira and Benissa, or the quiet inland valleys through the orange groves.

**Best towns for a winter base**

Not every town has the same winter personality. Here’s how they feel once the summer crowds have left:

**Dénia** — Best for year-round buzz and foodies. A proper working town and UNESCO City of Gastronomy that stays open 12 months a year. Calle Loreto and Marqués de Campo still hum on weekends. Stay near the centre, and you genuinely don’t need a car. [Denia Winter Deals](https://go.stay22.com/sunseasangria1/78MmrWRjUU)

**Jávea / Xàbia** — Best for expats and social clubs. Three distinct areas (Old Town, Port, Arenal) and a large, active winter expat community. If you want walking groups, padel, and live music nights through January, this is your spot. [Javea Winter Deals](https://go.stay22.com/sunseasangria1/hjfNrCn1PB)

**Calpe** — Best for flat walks and winter sun. Sheltered by the Peñón de Ifach, so it catches brilliant winter light. Long, flat beachfront promenades and plenty of apartments with sea views. Ideal if you want easy daily walks without climbing.[ Calpe Winter Deals](https://go.stay22.com/sunseasangria1/LYlwy06JHh)

**Moraira & Altea** — Best for quiet charm. Smaller, prettier and noticeably quieter. Plenty of local favourites stay open for the regulars who come back every year. [Moraira Winter Deals](https://go.stay22.com/sunseasangria1/pdYWmqQ8Bp)

**Travelling in a van or motorhome?**

A lot of people turn up here for the winter in a van, and the North works well for it once you know the options. Many of the proper campsites stay open year-round (or at least through the main winter months), and some offer monthly rates once the summer peak has gone. A few of the ones locals and regulars actually use:

* [Camping Los Pinos (Dénia)](https://www.lospinosdenia.com/) — Small, friendly, close to Montgó and the coast. Popular with people who want quiet and a bit of nature without being in the middle of nowhere.
* [Camping El Naranjal Javea ](https://www.campingelnaranjal.com/)— Handy for the Arenal, the old town and the walking trails. Good facilities and a decent winter crowd.
* [Paraíso Camper (Calpe)](https://www.paraisocamper.com/)— Flat and practical, with easy access to town and the promenade. Calpe’s winter sun and the Peñón views make it an easy base.
* [Camping Cap Blanch (Altea) ](https://www.camping-capblanch.com/)— Right by the beach if you like rolling out of the van onto the sand. Quieter in winter than further south.

Reality check: even in winter you still need to book the better-known sites if you want a decent pitch for more than a few nights. Some places also have dedicated motorhome service areas if you just need a night or two. Always double-check opening dates and whether they do long-stay rates; they can change.

**Private villas & apartments**

Around the Costa Blanca North, you’ll also find plenty of private villas and apartments with winter long-stay deals. Weekly and monthly rates drop a lot once summer ends, and many owners are happy to do 1–3 month lets. It’s a good middle option if you want your own space but don’t fancy a campsite.

One practical note: always check that the rental is properly registered with the Valencian tourist board, and take a good look at recent reviews before you book. A few extra minutes of checking saves a lot of hassle later.

**Practical tips before you book**

1. Check heating and orientation. Never book a winter rental without asking two things: does it get direct afternoon sun (south-facing is ideal)? And what kind of heating does it have? Air-con units with a heat pump (*bomba de calor*) are the gold standard. Be careful if it’s only log or pellet; depending on the exact location, wood and pellets can be surprisingly hard to find, and your first night can feel chilly.
2. Mind the 90/180-day rule. If you’re coming from the UK or outside the EU, keep a close eye on your Schengen days so you don’t accidentally overstay.
3. Rent a car for off-season rates. Winter car hire from Alicante or Valencia airport is a fraction of summer prices, often just a few euros a day plus insurance. Perfect for heading inland to Jalón (Xaló) when almond blossoms appear in late January and February. [Winter Car Rental Deals](https://www.travelsupermarket.com/en-gb/car-hire/spain/alicante/)
4. Get into the markets. Dénia on Mondays, Jávea on Thursdays, Calpe on Saturdays. Fresh winter oranges, avocados and local produce for almost nothing.

**Local transport (winter reality check)**

Public transport works, but it’s not seamless if you’re basing yourself for weeks or months. A car (or a van) still makes life much easier, especially once you’re off the main strip.

**TRAM d’Alacant** (the best option) Line 9 runs from Benidorm to Dénia, stopping at Altea, Calpe, Benissa, Teulada and Gata. It runs roughly hourly in both directions. To get further south to Alicante, you change at Benidorm onto Line 1. Scenic, cheap (a few euros depending on zones), and reliable. Reality check: Calpe station is a walk (or local bus) from the centre. Moraira has no station; you have to go to Teulada. Frequencies drop a bit in the evening and on Sundays. Always check the FGV/TRAM app or site for the exact day.

[Tram Alacant Schedule and Destinations](https://www.tramalacant.es/)

**Buses**

**Alsa** buses run the main inter-town and airport routes (Alicante/Alicante Airport ↔ Calpe/Dénia/Jávea, plus connections via Benidorm). Direct buses from Alicante to Dénia take about 1h 25–1h 35 and run several times a day. Local town buses exist (Bus Dénia has a decent network, including coastal routes; Calpe and others have smaller services). Winter timetables are reduced compared with summer. Airport buses are handy on arrival day but less practical for daily use.

[Alsa Bus Schedule and Tickets](https://www.alsa.com/en/)

**Taxis & ride apps:** Easy enough in the bigger towns. Prices are expensive here and can add up if you’re doing it regularly.

**Bottom line for a long stay**: Stay in Dénia or near the TRAM, and you can manage without a car for many days. Anywhere else (especially Moraira, inland, or if you want markets + beaches + walks), a rental car for the winter is still the most practical option, and winter hire rates from Alicante or Valencia are usually excellent.
